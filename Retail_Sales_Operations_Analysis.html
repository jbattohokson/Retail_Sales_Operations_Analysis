Executive Summary

This is a case study dataset from a bike and accessories retailer selling in the US, France, Germany and the UK (UK rows have no state, so the state-level analysis covers the other three). Monthly revenue averaged about $45K from December 2014 through July 2015, then jumped to about $440K in August 2015 and averaged roughly $470K a month through June 2016. The jump held, but it isn't organic growth: until late June 2015 the data contains only one sub-category, Road Bikes, and 11 more first appear between June 28 and July 12, 2015. Comparing the same 12 sub-categories month to month from August 2015 to June 2016, revenue is flat (+$5.8K a month, p = 0.33).

The jump hid a margin problem. US Bikes ran a -2.1% gross margin: a $9.8K gross loss on the $472K of US bike revenue that has a recorded cost. Germany sold the same categories at 16.7% on bikes.

The fix starts with US bike pricing. Accessories, already the largest US category at $2.35M, carry up to 22% margin on Fenders and Bike Racks and are the natural add-on at bike checkout.

Analysis

What the data shows

01The August 2015 jump is the catalog filling in, not growth

Revenue stayed under $55K a month from December 2014 through June 2015, rose to about $145K in July, then reached about $440K in August. It stayed between roughly $355K and $650K every month after that.

The data shows why. Through the week of June 21, 2015, Road Bikes is the only sub-category with sales. Bottles and Cages, Caps, Helmets, Jerseys, Mountain Bikes, Socks and Tires and Tubes first sell the week of June 28, then Cleaners, Hydration Packs, Vests and Shorts in early July. Fenders, Gloves and Bike Racks start in late December 2015 and January 2016.

July 2016 drops to about $210K, and its last week has only 25 transaction rows, so the month is likely incomplete. It's excluded from the like-for-like trend.

Interpretation

The jump is a range expansion (or the point where those lines start being recorded), not demand growth. Holding the product range fixed, the 12 sub-categories sold in every month from August 2015 to June 2016 show no significant trend. Use the post-August level as the forecasting baseline, and ask finance whether the pre-July data is complete before treating it as history.

02US bikes lose money at the gross profit line

US Bikes brought in roughly $570K at a -2.1% gross margin, about $10K in gross losses before operating costs. $101K of that revenue (18%) has no recorded cost, so the margin is measured on the $472K that does. Bikes are a small share of US unit volume (about 1,800 bikes vs. 20,000 accessories), but they carry the highest ticket price, and on average they sold below cost.

What this means for pricing

A US bike pricing review is the highest-value action in this dataset. The question for the pricing team: what increase on US bike SKUs reaches breakeven at current volume, and where does lost volume start to offset it? Germany's 16.7% bike margin on the same categories points to a US pricing or cost problem, not something built into the category.

03Accessories carry the margin, and the best ones are under-sold

Accessories are the largest US category by revenue ($2.35M) and units. Fenders (21.5%) and Bike Racks (21.9%) have the highest margins of any sub-category but small revenue: about $280K and $26K across all markets.

What this means for merchandising

Offering Fenders and Bike Racks at bike checkout raises blended margin without a price change. The direct upside is modest: a 10% attach rate on ~1,800 US bikes at $50 each is about $9,000 in revenue and roughly $1,300 in gross profit at the US accessories margin. Pricing is the bigger lever. The attach offer is a low-cost test to run alongside it.

04Germany beats the US on margin by 15+ points in every category

Germany's margins run 30.2% on Accessories and 28.5% on Clothing, about double the US in both, and 16.7% on Bikes vs. -2.1%. That's an 18.8-point gap on bikes alone.

What to find out next

The gap comes from price, cost or product mix, and each points to a different fix. Higher German prices on the same SKUs mean US underpricing. Lower German costs (freight, distribution, duties) mean an operations opportunity. A richer accessories mix means a merchandising opportunity. Pulling unit price and unit cost by SKU and market would separate the three.

0525 to 34 year olds drive volume, and gender splits are close to even

The 25 to 34 band is the largest at about $1.94M, roughly 20% above 35 to 44 (about $1.61M). Revenue falls off sharply after 55.

What this means for targeting

The regression (M2) backs up age but not gender. At the same quantity and product category, 25 to 34 segments bring in about $15K more revenue than 17 to 24 (p = 0.01), which points to a pricier mix. Gender adds nothing (p = 0.54). Women make up a slightly larger share of revenue from age 45 up, but that is a hypothesis to test, not a targeting rule. An A/B test of the checkout accessories offer by segment would show whether any group responds differently.

Statistical Models

Two OLS models and their limits

M1Time series: does revenue trend up over time?

A linear regression of monthly revenue on a time index, fit two ways.

Full period (all sub-categories, Dec 2014 to Jul 2016): R² = 0.674Slope = +$30,760 / monthp < 0.001Durbin-Watson 0.91n = 20 months

Like-for-like (the 12 sub-categories sold every month, Aug 2015 to Jun 2016): R² = 0.104Slope = +$5,764 / monthp = 0.33Durbin-Watson 2.08n = 11 months

Read before using these numbers

The full-period residuals run negative, then positive, then negative (Durbin-Watson 0.91), so that line is forced through two regimes. Its slope mostly measures new sub-categories arriving. Holding the range fixed, there's no significant trend.

M2Demographic model: what drives revenue per transaction?

Revenue per segment (age band x gender x sub-category, 174 cells) regressed on quantity and product category, then with age band and gender added, all one-hot encoded.

R² 0.782 → 0.802F-test for age + gender: p = 0.01625-34 vs 17-24: +$15.0K, p = 0.013Male vs female: p = 0.54

Limitation

Demographics add a little beyond quantity and category, almost all of it from the 25 to 34 band, and the result holds with heteroskedasticity-robust errors. It's cross-sectional and observational, so it says who buys the pricier mix, not what would change their behavior.
